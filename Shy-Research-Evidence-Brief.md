# Evidence Brief — Why People Don't Approach Strangers at Conferences

*Distilled by Muse from Claude's secondary-research report (chat "会议中陌生人搭讪研究",
https://claude.ai/chat/2ec746bc-8626-49f8-abba-96da20607a83, retrieved 2026-11-14).
Full report with per-claim sources and reliability labels lives in that chat.
This brief maps the evidence onto our 5-step conference chain and flags what may
and may not be said in the pitch.*

## The headline finding

The barrier is a **misperception located before the conversation starts**: people
systematically underestimate strangers' willingness to talk and overestimate
rejection. Once a conversation starts, it usually goes better than expected.
Shyness/social anxiety amplifies a universal misperception; it is not a niche
trait problem (social anxiety disorder 12-month prevalence ~7.1%, Ruscio et al.
2008, NCS-R; the over-worry appears in general samples, Sandstrom & Boothby 2021,
7 studies, N=2,304).

## What this means for each step of our chain

1. **Matching (who)** — supported indirectly: a pre-event recommendation list
   changed real behaviour in a conference RCT (Bapna & Funk 2021: women met +57%
   more new contacts, +90% conversation time; **no effect for men** — quote it
   with that caveat or don't quote it).
2. **Availability (is he free/open)** — **NO controlled study has ever tested an
   availability/open-to-chat signal directly.** Supporting evidence is practice
   records (red/yellow/green badges since 1996, COVID wristbands) and one adjacent
   lab finding: explicit permission raised speaking-up from ~20% to 62–71%
   (Sandstrom & Boothby 2021 — cross-study comparison, NOT a randomised trial;
   phrase it that way). **Pitch rule: present the signal as a mechanism-backed
   hypothesis we are validating, never as proven.**
3. **Invitation/accept (permission to walk over)** — the strongest adjacent
   evidence is mutual-introduction (Bapna & Funk: double opt-in introductions
   raised women's conversation time +66%). The risk evidence is concrete and must
   shape design: **>50% of connection/meeting requests on event apps go
   unanswered** (Swapcard platform logs, 9M+ requests; Brella's own figure ≈ 40%
   accepted for in-person meetings), and **being ignored produces measurable
   ostracism pain, hitting high-social-anxiety users hardest** — i.e., our exact
   target user is the one most harmed by a visible non-response. This is the
   empirical foundation for our silent-expiry / no-visible-rejection rule: it is
   load-bearing, not decoration.
4. **Meeting point (where)** — no direct research found. Indirect: scheduled 1:1
   no-show rates have NO published peer-reviewed or platform data; vendors admit
   "ghost meetings" exist. Our immediate, in-event meeting (minutes, not days
   later) is a reasonable countermeasure but is itself untested — say so.
5. **Conversation (how)** — tips alone don't fix it: "just give conversation tips"
   did not reduce approach worries (Sandstrom & Boothby 2021). What works in the
   literature is structure + reciprocity + follow-ups (Aron 1997; Huang 2017) —
   consistent with the four-card arc. Also: only ~2% of conversations end when
   both parties want them to (Mastroianni et al. 2021, 932 conversations) — the
   exit problem is real, supporting the designed closing card.

**Status staleness warning (design input):** IM research shows manual presence
status goes stale and is sometimes deliberately faked (Hancock et al.; Wu et al.
2021) — empirical support for our ~15-minute auto-expiry and schedule-linked
auto-off.

## Pitch-usable facts (Claude's List A — peer-reviewed, safe with citations)

1. 95% of guests at an executive reception said meeting new people was their goal,
   yet half their conversations were with people they already knew (Ingram &
   Morris 2007, ASQ, electronic-badge measurement, ~100 executives).
2. Social anxiety disorder: 12-month prevalence 7.1% (Ruscio et al. 2008) — but
   over-worry about stranger conversation is general-population (Sandstrom &
   Boothby 2021, N=2,304).
3. Liking gap: after talking, people systematically underestimate how much the
   other person liked them (Boothby et al. 2018, Psychological Science, 5 studies).
4. People fear the *start*, not the conversation: commuters predicted talking
   would be unpleasant because the other person seemed uninterested; those who
   talked enjoyed it more, and so did the people they talked to (Epley &
   Schroeder 2014; Schroeder et al. 2022).
5. Rejection is rarer than feared: in a week-long approach task, 87% of 1,336
   conversations were with the first person approached (Sandstrom et al. 2022).
6. A recommendation list changed conference behaviour (Bapna & Funk 2021, RCT):
   women +57% new contacts, +90% talk time — **no effect for men; disclose this.**
7. Randomly assigned 90-minute meetings raised later grant co-application by 75%
   (Boudreau et al. 2017, ~400 researchers).
8. Explicit permission to talk: 20% → 62–71% chose to talk (Sandstrom & Boothby
   2021; cross-study comparison — do not call it an RCT).

Background-only (industry surveys, label them as such): ~31% of attendees find
conference networking awkward (Freeman 2025, n=4,114); >50% of event-app requests
unanswered (Swapcard, 9M+ requests).

## Do NOT use (Claude's List B — unverifiable, checked)

- "Brella AI meetings have an 85% acceptance rate" — contradicts Brella's own ~40%.
- "87% exchange contacts with 10+ people / 3% become meaningful / 12% follow up
  within a week" (attributed to an Event Marketing Institute 2025 survey that
  could not be found).
- "83% attend mainly to network but leave disappointed"; "80% gain a valuable
  contact"; "40% feel uncomfortable at social events" (Keevee compilation, no
  original survey); "76% attend to meet peers."
- No-show/fulfilment benchmarks ("80% fulfilment", "free events 40–60% no-show",
  "booked a week ahead 23% no-show vs same-day 7%") — vendor blogs, no data.
- "223% more likely to meet someone they know" (Ingram & Morris distortion);
  "small groups raise collaboration 8×" (not in the Zajdela preprint);
  "shyness rose from 40% to 48%" (48% has no published method).

## Known gaps (say them before a judge does)

- No direct data on "attendees who met almost nobody new" or on conference
  loneliness — the team would have to survey this itself.
- No controlled test of availability signals exists (see step 2).
- No published 1:1 no-show rate for conferences.
- Almost all psychology samples are US/UK; no East Asian conference studies found.
